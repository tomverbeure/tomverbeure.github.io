# Introduction

A little of a month ago, I ran into a Tektronix TDS7104 at the
[Silicon Valley Flea Market](https://www.electronicsfleamarket.com/),
where else?

![TDS7104 in the trunk of my car](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_in_car.jpg)

Other than a few dirty buttons, a few smudges here and there, and the usual
assortment of calibration and asset tracking tags, the unit was in excellent 
cosmetic shape, but the price tag of $700 was way out of line: as I write this 
a try-before-you-buy TDS7104 can be had on Craigslist for the same price.

But don't dispair, Paul, the seller, is a regular and has a habit of saying 
"I'll make you a deal" and he did before I even asked: $300. That's still a lot
by flea market standards, but a pretty good price for a TDS7104... if you can get 
it to work.

I powered on the scope right after came home and it booted straight into the main
scope application! Other than a screen that was way too dim, everything seemed
to work.

![TDS7104 at first power up](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_first_powerup.jpg)

However, when I tried it again a few hours later, it got stuck at the BIOS screen 
with a CMOS battery error. 

![TDS7104 bootup error](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_cmos_error.jpg)

In this blog post, I go over the step I took to get the scope back in top shape.

# Why the TDS7104?

I've been gradually increasing the maximum specifications of my oscilloscopes because
the day will come where I'll need them for sure.

My Tektronix TDS794D is the bandwidth leader with 2 GHz at 4 Gs/s but it needs some serious
amount of tender loving care before it can be deployed in the lab: the gorgeous color CRT is 
broken and must be replaced with an LCD panel, and booting up is hit or miss due to an almost
empty battery backup RAM.  It's a nice scope, but a bit limited in terms of fancy 
measurement features and I/O interface, no Ethernet, and it only accepts 50 Ohm probes.

My TDS684B has a 1 GHz bandwidth at 5 Gs/s, but its cool 
[CCD based acquistion architecture](/2025/05/04/TDS684B-CCD-Memory.html)
comes with trigger and recording length limitations. Its backup battery RAM is also
on its last legs. This scope will be sold eventually.

Earlier this year, I modded a 
[600MHz Agilent 54831 to 1 GHz](/2026/03/28/Repair-of-Two-Agilent-54831-Oscilloscopes.html#upgrade-to-1-ghz-bandwidth)
at 4 Gs/s. This scope has some advanced measurement features such a jitter
analysis but it lacks a touch screen so you're condemned to a mouse if you want to use
anything more than the most basic features.

So except for the 2 GHz BW of the TDS794D, the TDS7104 is my new king of the hill: 
1 GHz bandwidth, a single channel sample rate of 10 Gs/s that drops down to 2.5 Gs/s
when recording 4 channels in parallel. There are advanced signal analysis and compliance
test features if you have the right license key, and in addition to plenty of physical
buttons, it has a resistive touch screen that is not horrible.

Like the 54832, the TDS7104 has a PC motherboard and runs Windows 2000 Pro Embedded
as general operating system.

# Common Failures

As always, you'll find a bunch of hobbyist trying to revive this kind of scope on the
EEVblog forum, Youtube and some blogs. Here are the most common failures:

* PC motherboard CMOS backup battery dead 
* PowerPC backup battery dead
* Harddrive dead
* Power suppy capacitors leaking

# Make an Image of the Hard Drive

Whether the machine boot or not, your first step should always first make an image
of the hard drive, a 6 GB IBM Travelstar in my case. 
[Like the 54831](/2026/03/28/Repair-of-Two-Agilent-54831-Oscilloscopes.html#first-suspect-the-ibm-travelstar-hd), 
I thought that I'd have to open the case to access the drive, but no, just push
on the spring-loaded black cover in the back and pull the drive sled out.

![TDS7104 hard drive sled](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_drive_sled.jpg)

Take the drive out of the sled, plug it into a 
[USB-to-IDE adapter](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08KT3F998), and extract the data.
On Windows, I use [HDD Raw Copy Tool](https://hddguru.com/software/HDD-Raw-Copy-Tool/).

# Do NOT Remove the Front Panel

I'm putting this section before the Disassembly one to make sure those with low attention
span get the message: **chances are high that you don't need to remove the front panel**.

And that's a good because, unlike the TDSxxx series scopes, the front panel has 
some plastic tabs are very easy to break. That said, even if you do break them (I did!),
the result is not catastrophic and you should be able to put it back firmly where it belongs 
with no one noticing a thing.

[![Service manual figure 6-3: Trim Removal](/assets/tds7104/service_manual_trim_removal.jpg)](/assets/tds7104/service_manual_trim_removal.jpg)
*(Click to Enlarge)*

The [TDS7000 Series Service Manual](https://www.tek.com/en/oscilloscope/tds7054-manual/tds7000-series-service-manual)
makes it sound easy enough:

> To remove the trim ring, slide the flat end of a soldering aid into the side
> slot on the trim ring. Press in, then lift up to hook it underneath, then pry up.

And from the pictures, it's as if you can remove the front panel without removing anything
else. That just didn't work...

The front panel consists of multiple click tabs: 1 on the left side, 1 on the right and then
a bunch at the top and the bottom. So far so good. However, the left and right side also
have 2 slide tabs that go into the metal rails. If you lift the left and right tabs to much,
these plastic slide tabs break off.

So you need to be very careful in make sure that you don't lift the plastic trim too much,
and that you slide the panel out while it stays parallel with the display.

Or... you don't touch it: you can do all PC maintenance, including replacing the floppy drive,
without removing the front panel.

# Disassembly

I will continue my tradition of documenting the disassembly of test equipment in detail
because *nobody else does it*. Even though the service manual technically describes how
to do it, a few pictures can go a long way to make it easier.

To access the inside of the scope, you need to remove more than 30 screws. On the plus
side, they're all Torx-15 screws and they are all the same length, so you don't need
to worry about keeping track of which screw goes where.

Still, it takes a while and it validated my recent purchase of this
[cordless screwdriver](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07L78Y72J), recommened by Shirar
over at The SignalPath.

**Unbutton the accessory bag**

![Remove the accessory bag](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_remove_bag.jpg)

This took me longer to figure out that I want to admit: you can just unclick the bag
from the chassis, but the buttons can be very tight and if you're not caseful you can
tear the fabric. Use a flat-head screwdriver right next to each button to pop it off.

**Put the scope upright on its back feet**

It's an unusual arrangment, but the easiest way to dismantle the scope is by putting it
on its back feet: you don't need to remove any screw from the back!

![TDS7104 on its back feet](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_on_its_back_feet.jpg)

Let me once again sing the praises of a sturdy equipment cart: it's so much easier to
walk around or move the cart than to muscle around bulky, heavy test equipment on a table.

**Remove the top panel**

4 screws through the accessory bag buttons fix the top panel to the chassis.

![TDS7104 remove top panel](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_top_panel.jpg)

After removing this panel, you could remove the side panels if you can, but I found it
much easier to remove the bottom panel next.

**Remove the bottom panel and loosen the front connector enclosure**

The 5 screws of bottom panel are next as well as 3 screws that keep the black enclosure
of the front BNC connectors in place.

![TDS7104 bottom panel and connector enclosure](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_back_panel.jpg)

The black enclosure doesn't need to be completely removed, only loosened because otherwise
it will soon be in the way of some other screws.

The bottom panel shall now be removed though. Just slide it down a bit and take it off.

*In the picture above, you can see 2 screws that aren't marked in red. That's because they
don't keep the bottom panel in place. But if you feel like it, you might as well remove them
now too.*

**Remove the handle and side panels**

![TDS7104 side panel with handle](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_side_panel.jpg)

With the bottom panel gone, the side panels are a breeze to remove, after unscrewing the 
handle.

**Remove the 2 sheet metal parts**

With the outer covers removed, you're now starting the sheet metal RF protection
enclosure. It consists of 2 parts, each part covers 2 sides. Remove all the screws,
then first take off the bottom part and then the top.

Note how some of the bottom screws are hidden underneath the BNC connector cover. That's
why you had to remove its 3 screws!

![TDS7104 sheet metal top](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_sheet_top.jpg)

![TDS7104 sheet metal right](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_sheet_right.jpg)

![TDS7104 sheet metal bottom](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_sheet_bottom.jpg)

![TDS7104 sheet metal left](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_sheet_left.jpg)

Congratulations! For those who didn't keep track: you've removed 32 screws!

After removing the panels, you now have access to the acquisition board
at the bottom and the PC motherboard at the top:

![TDS7104 acquisition board](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_acquisition_board.jpg)

![TDS7104 PC motherboard](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_pc_motherboard.jpg)

One side has nothing but cooling fans, but from the other size you can see the
power supply and an RS-232 port that you will need to connect if the backup battery
of the PowerPC controller board expires. 

![TDS7104 right side](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_right_inside.jpg)

If you need access to those items, you've only done the easy disassembly part. On
my unit, both the PSU and the controller backup battery were fine so I didn't have
to do the hard part.

*Note on the picture above that the front panel has been removed. You do NOT have
to do this for pretty much all restoration cases! You really shouldn't.*

**Reinstall the bottom sheet metal cover**

All of my work on the scope was on the PC motherboard and I had to put the scope
back in its horizontal position. To make sure that I didn't accidentally damage
the acquistion board, I put the bottom sheet metal cover back in its place.

![TDS7104 bottom sheet metal back in place](/assets/tds7104/tds7104_bottom_sheet_back.jpg)

# Winning recipe


## Create a Win2K Pro USB Stick

* Download Win2K Pro ISO from the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/win2kproiso)
* Mount the ISO on your Windows PC with File Explorer
* Install and run WinSetupFromUSB to install the Win2K Pro ISO onto a bootable USB stick
* Select the USB stick as targer
* Auto format with FBinst, use FAT32 file system
* Add to USB disk: Windows 2000/XP/2003 Setup
* Select the mounted Win2K Pro ISO drive as source 
* Do "GO" to copy Win2K Pro onto the USB stick

## Create a Plop boot manager floppy for the TDS7104 to boot from

* RadiSys SF810 motherboard doesn't support booting from a USB stick
* My CDROM doesn't work
* [Download Plop](https://www.plop.at/en/bootmanager/download.html)
* Unzip the file. Look for the 1.44 MB plop.img file
* Use Rawrite32 (Win10 and later only) or Rawrite for Windows (win32 exe) to copy the img file to a floppy

## Win2K Pro First Stage installation

* Insert USB stick with Win2K Pro ISO in the lonely scope USB port
* Do NOT use a USB hub: Plop doesn't support that!
* Insert Plop floppy
* Insert new SSD in the scope
* Set BIOS menu so that the scope boots first from floppy
* Boot from floppy
* Select booting from USB
* Select phase 1 Win2K installation from the menu
* Follow instructions...
* Format SSD drive
* Remove floppy disk from drive
* Reboot

## Win2K Pro Second Stage installation

* Should be booting from the SSD now
* It will take a while but eventually, the setup will require the Win2K USB stick.
  If you don't have a PS/2 mouse, use a hub to connect both mouse and USB stick. It should work.
* Follow instructions.
* Eventually a reboot will happen and Windows will work

You now need to install Tektronix specific drivers...
